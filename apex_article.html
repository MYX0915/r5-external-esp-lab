Update 2026/11/04: As someone who has previously dealt with EAC, i had my share of troubles when encountering some of EA’s tricks. In order to illustrate how funny it can get, let me introduce you to one of their memes. For this one, they will periodically overwrite the prologue of ntoskrnl’s MmCopyMemory function, while maintaining the hook for a relatively short period of time, before they then retire it by restoring the original bytes in the function prologue. When i checked who is writing to it, it was a generic memmove-like function inside eaanticheat.sys. For both the patch and restore operation. I’m not claiming i know exactly what they use it for, but it’s still interesting to note down.

What do they overwrite it with, you might ask? Let’s see for ourselves:

ntoskrnl.exe!MmCopyMemory:

Original: 48 89 5C 24 20 55 56 57 41 54 41 55
ASM:
mov [rsp+20h], rbx

This is a classic way to redirect the program counter. It moves the destination into RAX register and then jumps to the contents of RAX. Which in this case, seems to be their hook handler for this specific target. Below is some pseudo code, beautified with GPT:

        EBP = Flags;

        R15 = OriginalCallerReturnAddress;

The issue would not even be finding or disarming their hooks. For this specific technique of EA, you could do the following:

  1. Take a look at your ntoskrnl.exe, which can be different across OS builds. Locate the MmCopyMemory function.
  2. If you’re on Windows 10, you will most likely see a bunch of MOV and PUSH instructions at its prologue.
  3. If you don’t need it to support different OS builds, you can now simply build a little stub that basically simulates the real prologue of MmCopyMemory, and then jump to the actual MmCopyMemory function with an offset, so that you don’t execute the first 12 bytes at all on the real one. Since we know exactly which gadget they’re using, it’s quite easy.
  4. So, the flow basically is: you invoke your MmCopyMemory wrapper —> it runs the same instructions of the known-good prologue that you have recovered from your ntoskrnl —> then jumps to ( ntoskrnl.MmCopyMemory + <instructions to skip from the potentially patched real prologue>. The idea is that you’d execute enough of the prologue that you can skip EA’s hook entirely, and jump a little further than their meme inline hook, so if you call your wrapper it won’t trigger it at all.

Even though you could detect this too, one could simply stop relying on this API for memory access, and use other means to achieve the same thing. Or simply disable EA’s hook, check if its hooked by reading the prologue, restore the site around the patched function to a known good value, right before calling it, etc… However these would mostly just be a fat race condition. And it’s unnecessary unless EA starts hooking the mid or end of a function, or deploys more sophisticated methods of intercepting execution.

Part 2: Possible removal of Theia

I don’t know if they completely got rid of everything related to Theia, which used to be their anti-tamper/packer hybrid solution. But Apex seems to no longer be using a dynamic scheme for keeping stale code pages encrypted. Previously, you’d have to deal with incomplete dumps and a lot of issues with your disassembler, due to scenarios like a page in the midst of others being encoded, while the others are in decoded state. So if your using something like IDA Pro, it might confuse perfectly valid code that is surrounding this encoded block as data, even if it is perfectly valid instructions.

I previously dealt with this by building a script that attempts to automate this process of marking these adjacent valid instructions as code. It worked fine for thwe most part, and with some manual intervention here and there, it was mostly manageable. Most of the time, if i had a goal of understanding how something works, i was able to achieve it using my old method of dumping. It was everything BUT convenient, but it was manageable. Even if sometimes i had important code in these cold pages which would have been a blessing to be allowed to inspect.

When it comes to attacking Theia itself or leveraging a flaw within its components and their design, i have tried it but ultimately gave up. In my case, my tooling and current knowledge was simply not mature enough to progress in reasonable speed while working on understanding it. I was able to reconstruct some parts like their exception handling mechanism, and some of the other protections they have offered. Also taken a look at their hardware identification methods, and inspected some interesting system calls they were making. Some of it has lead to a dead end, while others have taught me a lot of useful knowledge about it.
